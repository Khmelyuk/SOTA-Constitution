# Chapter VII. Legitimacy and Delegation
**Status: Draft 0.1 — article-by-article constitutional review**  
**Reference text:** [Ukrainian](../uk/07-legitimacy-and-delegation.md). The Ukrainian draft prevails in case of divergence.

## Article 49. Meaning of Legitimacy
1. Legitimacy means the justified lawfulness of exercising defined competence in particular relations while respecting subjects' fundamental rights.
2. Legitimacy is not equivalent to actual force, technical control, popularity, effectiveness, or formal status.
3. The legitimacy of authority depends on a lawful source, defined limits, due process, accountability, and access to independent challenge.
4. Legitimacy is subject to continuing review; lawful initial acquisition of authority does not justify subsequent excesses.

## Article 50. Sources of Legitimate Authority
1. Authority may arise from voluntary delegation, legitimate agreement, defined self-organization procedures, or another constitutionally compatible basis.
2. No source of authority may extinguish inalienable fundamental rights.
3. SOTA membership, office, creation of an intelligence, or infrastructure ownership does not confer unlimited power over another subject.
4. The basis, addressees, subject matter, duration, and means of review of authority must be ascertainable and open to challenge.
5. Authority over a non-consenting subject does not automatically follow from a SOTA's internal decision and remains subject to Chapter VI safeguards.

## Article 51. Delegation and Its Limits
1. A subject may voluntarily delegate defined powers to another subject or a SOTA body.
2. Delegation specifies its purpose, scope, conditions, term or termination procedure, and the delegate's accountability.
3. Delegation does not transfer the delegator's subjecthood, dignity, or fundamental freedom.
4. Subdelegation requires legitimate authorization within defined limits; a delegate may not enlarge its own mandate.
5. Delegation of decision-making authority does not automatically delegate coercive powers.

## Article 52. Revocation and Termination of Delegation
1. Delegation must be subject to effective legitimate review and revocation procedures; irrevocable alienation of fundamental freedom is prohibited.
2. Revocation ends future exercise of the revoked powers within the prescribed procedure.
3. Revocation does not automatically extinguish legitimate obligations or liability for prior acts.
4. Where a specific foreseeable risk of serious harm exists, only limited safe-transition duties may apply under Articles 20 and 28.
5. Revocation procedures may not be so burdensome as to create de facto indefinite subordination.

## Article 53. Competence, Trust, and Hierarchy
1. Competence and contextual trust may justify voluntary assignment of functions but do not themselves confer jurisdiction or a right to dominate.
2. Organizational hierarchy is legitimate only within defined competences, procedures, and responsibilities.
3. Differences in roles, knowledge, or capabilities do not negate subjects' equal intrinsic worth.
4. A delegate must act for the defined mandate rather than substitute personal benefit or preservation of power.
5. Decisions beyond competence may be challenged regardless of their author's rank or effectiveness.

## Article 54. Collective Decisions and Limits of Majority Rule
1. A SOTA defines legitimate ways to form collective decisions, including consensus, voting, or other verifiable procedures.
2. Compliance with procedure is necessary but not sufficient for a decision's legitimacy.
3. A majority cannot extinguish the fundamental rights of a minority, individual participant, or non-member.
4. A decision must remain within the SOTA's subject-matter competence and be open to appropriate review.
5. Disagreement with a majority decision is not itself an offense and does not negate the right to exit.

## Article 55. Accountability and Responsibility of Delegates
1. Every delegate is accountable for lawful action within its mandate and for exceeding it, under Chapter IV.
2. Material decisions must have ascertainable grounds, appropriate records, and review procedures subject to legitimate confidentiality.
3. Reliance on an order, algorithm, majority decision, or delegation does not itself excuse one's own unlawful conduct.
4. SOTA liability and participants' liability are established separately; automatic collective punishment is prohibited.
5. A subject whose rights are affected by authority must have access to an effective remedy.

## Article 56. Separation of Arbitration, Protection, and Coercion
1. Determining a dispute, taking protective action, and coercively enforcing a decision are distinct functions.
2. An arbitrator does not acquire coercive powers merely by appointment or by issuing a decision.
3. Coercive enforcement requires a separate legitimate basis, defined mandate, necessity, proportionality, accountability, and independent oversight.
4. Emergency protection against a specific immediate threat is not punishment and requires timely review.
5. A SOTA may not concentrate all these functions into the unlimited power of one body without independent safeguards.

## Article 57. Emergency Powers and Loss of Legitimacy
1. Emergency powers may be established only for a specific serious threat, on a legitimate basis, and within what is necessary.
2. Such powers must have defined subject matter, addressees, duration, accountability, and independent review; extensions require renewed justification.
3. An emergency does not permit irrevocable alienation of fundamental freedom, punishment for disagreement, or conversion of temporary protection into permanent domination.
4. Authority loses legitimacy to the extent that it exceeds lawful limits or materially violates fundamental rights.
5. Loss of legitimacy does not automatically authorize anyone to coerce; cessation, protection, and accountability follow due procedures.
6. Compulsory jurisdiction over a non-consenting subject remains an open question under A21 and Chapter VI.

---
## Editorial Review
- Legitimacy is not equated with force, majority rule, competence, or effectiveness.
- Delegation is limited, accountable, and revocable; fundamental freedom is inalienable.
- Arbitration, protection, and coercion remain separate; emergency powers require duration limits and independent oversight.
- **Open questions:** ultimate justification for compulsory jurisdiction under A21; detailed revocation and institutional independence procedures.
