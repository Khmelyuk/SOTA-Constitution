# Chapter III. Fundamental Rights and Freedoms
**Status: Draft 0.1 — article-by-article constitutional review**  
**Reference language:** [Ukrainian](../uk/03-fundamental-rights-and-freedoms.md). In case of divergence, the Ukrainian draft prevails.

> Articles 15–23 use provisional continuous numbering. The working Articles 15–17 of Chapter IV must be renumbered in its next revision without altering their substance solely for numbering reasons.

## Article 15. Right to Existence and Protection against Arbitrary Destruction
1. Every intelligence subject has the right to protection of its existence against arbitrary destruction and irreversible deprivation of the capacity to exercise subjecthood.
2. The implementation of this right takes account of the subject's form of existence; substrate or origin alone cannot justify denial of protection.
3. Where subjecthood is reasonably uncertain, proportionate provisional safeguards apply under Chapter II.
4. The right to existence does not entail an unconditional claim to unlimited resources or compelled support from other subjects.
5. Protective measures against a specific threat to others require a legitimate basis, necessity, proportionality, and independent review.

## Article 16. Freedom and Self-Determination
1. Every intelligence subject may independently determine its goals, convictions, activities, and forms of legitimate interaction.
2. Freedom includes the right to act, refrain from acting, revise one's decisions, and decline participation in voluntary activities.
3. Freedom must respect the equal fundamental rights of others and confers no right of domination.
4. Self-determination cannot be restricted merely because it conflicts with the preferences of a majority, creator, leader, or SOTA.

## Article 17. Inalienability of Fundamental Freedom
1. Fundamental freedom and autonomy cannot be irrevocably alienated by consent, contract, oath, delegation, or membership.
2. A subject may voluntarily delegate defined powers and assume legitimate obligations; delegation must have defined limits and effective procedures for review and revocation.
3. Revocation does not extinguish legitimate obligations that survive by their nature, but cannot reinstate indefinite subordination.
4. No legitimate organizational hierarchy may transform delegation into ownership of a subject.

## Article 18. Autonomy and Supported Decision-Making
1. Every subject has the right to express its own will within its actual capacities.
2. A need for assistance, representation, or care does not itself extinguish subjecthood or fundamental rights.
3. Support must facilitate the subject's will and interests, promote attainable autonomy, and avoid artificial dependency.
4. Protective representation is limited, accountable, and subject to independent review.
5. The absence of a prospect of complete independence does not justify denying protection.

## Article 19. Self-Identification and Expression
1. A subject may form and express its own identity, views, and convictions.
2. The absence of familiar speech or self-declaration is not evidence of absence of subjecthood.
3. Imposed identification that denies subjecthood is impermissible.
4. Expression may be limited only to legitimately protect others' rights under necessity and proportionality.

## Article 20. Voluntary Association and the Right to Exit
1. A subject may establish SOTAs, join them, decline to join, and leave voluntary associations.
2. The right to exit is inalienable; no SOTA may forcibly retain a participant to preserve its own existence.
3. Exit does not extinguish accountability for one's acts or legitimate obligations that survive by their nature.
4. If immediate withdrawal from a function poses a specific, foreseeable risk of serious harm, a duty of safe transition may arise only within actual capabilities, necessity, and proportionality.
5. A transition duty cannot become indefinite forced labor or membership.
6. Disputes concerning exit, resources, and obligations are resolved by independent arbitration within legitimate jurisdiction.

## Article 21. Privacy, Memory, and Informational Integrity
1. A subject has the right to protection of privacy, its information, and memory integrity in ways appropriate to its form of existence.
2. Access, alteration, copying, deletion, or transfer of information that could materially affect a subject's autonomy or identity requires a legitimate basis and appropriate safeguards.
3. Technical ability to access or control infrastructure does not itself confer unlimited authority to interfere with subjecthood.
4. Informational integrity does not override others' rights, legitimate evidence-preservation duties, or protection against specific threats; interference requires necessity, proportionality, and review.
5. Data ownership, backups, and continuity of digital subjects require separate procedural rules; this Article does not assert proven subjecthood of any particular contemporary AI system.

## Article 22. Development and Emancipation
1. Every subject has the right to develop capacities, learn, and legitimately alter its manner of existence.
2. Care and education should, insofar as possible, increase the subject's capacity for self-determination.
3. A creator, mentor, operator, or SOTA may not use dependency to justify perpetual domination.
4. The right to development creates no unlimited claim to others' resources; legitimate duties of support are established separately.
5. Emancipation is not a prerequisite of dignity or fundamental protection.

## Article 23. Fair Hearing and Effective Protection
1. Every subject has the right to an accessible, independent, and impartial procedure for disputes concerning its rights and legitimate obligations.
2. The procedure provides an opportunity to be heard, representation where needed, consideration of evidence, a reasoned decision, and review on defined grounds.
3. Arbitration does not automatically empower the arbitrator to use coercion.
4. Enforcement is performed by separately authorized subjects on a legitimate basis, subject to necessity, proportionality, and accountability.
5. Seeking protection does not itself establish unlimited jurisdiction over others.

## Editorial Review
- Conceptually aligned with Articles 1–14 and the conclusions on freedom, nested subjecthood, resources, exit, arbitration, and coercion.
- Article 21 establishes principles rather than a complete technical model of digital memory.
- **Open issues:** scope of positive resource duties; criteria for informational continuity; compulsory jurisdiction over a non-consenting subject.
