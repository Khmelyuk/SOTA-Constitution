# Chapter IV. Responsibility and Duties
**Status: Draft 0.4 — article-by-article constitutional review**  
**Continuous numbering:** Articles 24–30, following Articles 15–23 in Chapter III.  
**Reference text:** [Ukrainian](../uk/04-responsibility-and-duties.md). The Ukrainian draft governs in case of divergence.

## Article 24. Freedom of Choice and Responsibility for Consequences
1. An intelligence subject's freedom includes the right to act, refrain from acting, or observe without intervening.
2. Choosing to act, refrain, or observe is not, in itself, an offense.
3. Conduct is assessed with regard to its impact on others' rights, causation, foreseeability of risk, actual ability to affect outcomes, and legitimate duties.
4. A harmful outcome alone does not establish fault or legal liability.
5. An omission may incur liability only where a specific legitimate duty to act, a real capacity to perform it, and the other requirements of the applicable form of liability are established.
6. No subject is required to actively defend another merely because that other subject considers intervention desirable.

## Article 25. Shared Responsibility to Protect Fundamental Rights
1. Protection of fundamental rights is a shared constitutional responsibility of intelligence subjects irrespective of origin, jurisdiction, hierarchy, or SOTA membership.
2. Every subject may legitimately defend its own rights and those of others.
3. A specific duty to act arises from a legitimate rule, voluntarily assumed obligation, or other proper legal basis and takes account of competence, capabilities, risks, and others' rights.
4. Shared protective responsibility does not impose an unconditional duty of physical or technical intervention.
5. Emergency protection against an immediate threat of serious harm must be necessary, proportionate, limited to its protective purpose, and subject to independent review.
6. The right to protect does not itself confer arbitral powers, unlimited coercive authority, or domination over the protected subject.

## Article 26. Grounds and Limits of Legal Liability
1. Liability for an offense must rest on elements defined by a legitimate rule and established by adequate evidence.
2. Liability for a culpable offense requires identification of the subject, the protected interest, unlawful conduct, and the mental or other fault element specified by the rule; harm and causation must be proved where they are required elements.
3. A subject's ability to understand its conduct, foresee consequences, and control its actions is assessed in light of its nature and actual capacities; human standards must not be mechanically imposed on digital or collective forms.
4. Fault is not presumed from an adverse outcome, origin, creator status, SOTA membership, organizational role, or association with an offender.
5. Preventive safeguards, compensation, and punitive liability have distinct grounds and must not be conflated.
6. Punitive liability requires an independent fair process, a presumption of innocence, defense rights, and review.
7. No one may be punished for conduct not defined as an offense by a legitimate rule when committed; separately justified non-punitive duties to remedy or cease ongoing harm are not thereby excluded.

## Article 27. Legitimate Obligations and Limits of Performance
1. A subject may voluntarily assume defined legitimate obligations, including through contracts, membership, mandates, or delegation.
2. An obligation must have defined content and limits, a proper basis, and accessible dispute-resolution mechanisms.
3. No obligation may irrevocably alienate fundamental freedom or impose indefinite forced subordination.
4. Revocation of delegation, exit from a SOTA, or change of status does not automatically extinguish legitimate obligations that survive by their nature.
5. Performance may not require unlawful interference with others' fundamental rights.
6. Disputes over validity, scope, performance, or termination are subject to independent arbitration within legitimate jurisdiction.

## Article 28. Responsible Exit and Safe Transition
1. Exercising the inalienable right to leave a voluntary SOTA is not itself an offense.
2. Where a subject legitimately undertook a critical function whose immediate cessation creates a specific foreseeable risk of serious harm, a limited duty of notice, handover, or other safe transition may arise.
3. Such a duty depends on the subject's actual capabilities, the nature of the risk, necessity, proportionality, and a reasonable period.
4. A transition duty does not authorize indefinite forced membership or labor.
5. A SOTA must, within its competence, plan continuity of critical functions and may not shift its organizational failures onto a participant's fundamental freedom.
6. Outstanding property, contractual, and other legitimate obligations are resolved separately from the right to exit itself.

## Article 29. Responsibility of SOTAs and Their Participants
1. A SOTA, as an independent collective subject, may hold its own rights, duties, and liabilities within its legitimate activities.
2. A SOTA's liability is not identical to every participant's liability; membership, voting participation, or subordination alone does not prove an individual offense.
3. Individual liability depends on one's own acts or omissions, competence, fault, and other required grounds; liability of an organ or authorized executor is determined separately.
4. Punitive collective punishment of participants solely for SOTA membership is prohibited.
5. A SOTA answers for its obligations through its own or legitimately obtained resources; claims to participants' or third parties' resources do not arise automatically.
6. Exit, division, or dissolution may not be used to evade legitimate obligations; allocation and succession require legitimate settlement and, in case of dispute, arbitration.
7. Where no participants remain, a SOTA ceases to operate as a collective subject, but outstanding property rights and obligations do not automatically disappear.

## Article 30. Restoration, Compensation, and Limits of Coercion
1. Rights violations should, insofar as possible, be addressed by cessation, restoration, compensation for proven harm, or other legitimate remedies.
2. Restorative measures differ from punishment and take account of the violation, causation, responsible party's capabilities, and others' rights.
3. Arbitration may determine liability and appropriate remedies within legitimate jurisdiction but does not thereby acquire authority to use coercion itself.
4. Coercive enforcement requires a separate legitimate basis, duly delegated competence, necessity, proportionality, accountability, and independent review.
5. Liability may not be used to irrevocably deprive a subject of fundamental freedom or establish domination.
6. Legitimate preventive restrictions may protect against a specific threat but do not replace proof of fault or constitute punishment without due process.

## Editorial Review
- Articles 24–26 preserve and develop the former provisional Articles 15–17 of Ukrainian Working Draft 0.3.
- Articles 27–30 add obligations, responsible exit, separate SOTA liability, and remedies.
- Reflects inalienable freedom, resource autonomy, nested subjecthood, and separation of arbitration from coercion.
- **For later chapters:** succession procedure; exact criteria of collective liability; jurisdiction over non-members.
- **Status:** Draft 0.4, not formally adopted; full Cross-Axiom Check A01–A21 remains outstanding.
