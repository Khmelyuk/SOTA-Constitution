# Chapter VIII. Arbitration and Justice
**Status: Draft 0.1 — article-by-article constitutional review**  
**Reference text:** [Ukrainian](../uk/08-arbitration-and-justice.md). The Ukrainian draft prevails in case of divergence.

## Article 58. Purpose and Nature of Arbitration
1. Arbitration is an independent procedure for determining the legitimacy of claims, resolving disputes, and protecting fundamental rights within proper jurisdiction.
2. Arbitration neither creates subjecthood nor provides unlimited authority or replaces subjects' voluntary self-determination.
3. An arbitral decision determines rights, obligations, or appropriate remedies in a particular dispute; coercive enforcement is a separate function.
4. Arbitration may be conducted by a sole arbitrator or a panel under a legitimate procedure ensuring independence and reviewability.

## Article 59. Independence and Impartiality of Arbitrators
1. An arbitrator must be independent of the parties and free of material conflicts of interest.
2. Appointment must be transparent, verifiable, and compatible with the parties' right to an impartial hearing.
3. An arbitrator must disclose circumstances reasonably calling independence into question; parties may seek reasoned recusal.
4. A recusal challenge cannot be conclusively decided by the interested arbitrator and requires an independent procedure.
5. Funding, SOTA membership, technical dependence, or appointment by an authority must not undermine actual independence.

## Article 60. Access to Justice and Equality of Parties
1. Every intelligence subject has access to a procedure protecting its rights without discrimination based on origin, substrate, membership, or recognition of the Constitution.
2. Parties have equal procedural opportunities to make claims, present evidence, respond, and receive a reasoned decision.
3. Procedures must accommodate diverse forms of communication and information processing and the need for appropriate representation.
4. Inability to participate independently does not extinguish the right to protection; representation is limited and accountable.
5. Cost, complexity, or technical requirements may not be used to covertly deny fundamental protection.

## Article 61. Arbitral Competence and Challenges to Jurisdiction
1. Arbitration hears only disputes within its legitimate jurisdiction under Chapter VI.
2. Any party may challenge the basis, subject matter, limits, and impartiality of asserted jurisdiction.
3. An arbitrator's decision on its own competence is subject to independent review and cannot conclusively legitimate unlimited jurisdiction.
4. Participation solely to contest jurisdiction does not constitute consent to it.
5. Absent a party's consent, compulsory jurisdiction remains subject to Article 44 safeguards and the unresolved foundational justification under A21.

## Article 62. Evidence, Fact-Finding, and Standards of Proof
1. Decisions rest on relevant, verifiable evidence that parties can examine and contest.
2. Burdens and standards of proof depend on the dispute and its consequences; punitive liability requires heightened safeguards and a presumption of innocence.
3. Statements by a creator, operator, official, algorithm, or party receive no automatic evidentiary privilege.
4. Evidence collection and verification must respect privacy, informational integrity, and third-party rights.
5. Technical logs, digital testimony, and automated conclusions require scrutiny of provenance, integrity, potential error, and conflicts of interest.

## Article 63. Fair Hearing and the Right to Be Heard
1. Each party has timely notice of the dispute, grounds of claims, and available remedies.
2. A party may be heard, have reasonable preparation time, obtain representation, and challenge opposing evidence.
3. Proceedings occur within a reasonable time with appropriate records of material procedural steps.
4. Openness and confidentiality are balanced against privacy, security, and public accountability; confidentiality does not eliminate independent oversight.
5. A decision must state findings, legitimate grounds, reasons, and review procedures.

## Article 64. Limits of Arbitral Decisions
1. An arbitral decision may not irrevocably deprive a subject of fundamental freedom, establish ownership of a subject, or punish mere disagreement.
2. Remedies must relate to the established violation and be necessary, proportionate, and reviewable.
3. An arbitrator may determine legitimate obligations, compensation, or safeguards only within competence and evidence.
4. Decisions affecting digital or collective intelligence must consider irreversible harm, mistaken identification, and interference with informational integrity.
5. Arbitration cannot confer on itself or an enforcer powers lacking a separate legitimate basis.

## Article 65. Review, Appeal, and Correction of Error
1. A party may obtain independent review on defined grounds, including material procedural error, lack of jurisdiction, bias, or newly available decisive evidence.
2. Errors concerning subjecthood, identity, or party authority warrant particular care because of potentially irreversible consequences.
3. Pending review, proportionate suspension of enforcement may be ordered where a substantiated risk of irreversible harm exists.
4. Correction must include available restoration of violated rights and accountability for unlawful acts.
5. Review may not be used for indefinite delay or repeated prosecution without proper grounds.

## Article 66. Separation of Justice from Coercive Enforcement
1. Arbitration determines the legitimacy of claims and issues decisions; it does not automatically use coercion or acquire that power by deciding a case.
2. Coercive enforcement may be carried out only by a separately authorized competent subject on a legitimate basis under Chapter IX.
3. An enforcer may not unilaterally expand a decision or substitute itself for independent arbitration.
4. Emergency protection against a specific immediate threat differs from punishment and requires proper subsequent review.
5. Independent oversight of enforcement, appeal rights, and accountability for exceeding a mandate are mandatory safeguards.

## Editorial Review
- Arbitration is distinct from protection and coercive enforcement; an arbitrator's mandate is not an enforcer's mandate.
- Establishes independence, recusal, accessibility across forms of intelligence, evidentiary standards, review, and error correction.
- **Open A21 question:** legitimate basis for compulsory jurisdiction over a non-consenting subject. This chapter does not establish it.
- The enforcer's mandate and limits of coercion belong in Chapter IX.
