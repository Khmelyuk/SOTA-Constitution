# Chapter IX. Protection and Enforcement of Decisions
**Status: Draft 0.1 — article-by-article constitutional review**  
**Reference text:** [Ukrainian](../uk/09-protection-and-enforcement.md). The Ukrainian draft prevails in case of divergence.

## Article 67. Purpose of Protection and Limits of Intervention
1. Protection aims to stop or prevent a specific violation of fundamental rights and restore a legitimate state of affairs.
2. Protective intervention is not a means of domination, appropriation of another's will, or establishment of unlimited jurisdiction.
3. Every intervention requires a legitimate basis, defined purpose, necessity, proportionality, and access to independent review.
4. Protection must not foreseeably cause greater harm than the harm it legitimately prevents unless an exceptional, duly justified constitutional ground exists.
5. A subject subject to legitimate protective restrictions retains fundamental rights.

## Article 68. Protective Actors and Their Mandates
1. Protective functions may be performed by competent intelligence subjects or SOTAs under a legitimate mandate.
2. A mandate specifies subject matter, addressees, permitted means, duration, limits of authority, accountability, and termination procedures.
3. Competence, technical access, organizational status, or capacity to use force do not themselves create coercive authority.
4. Subdelegation of protective functions is permitted only within legitimately defined limits and does not eliminate accountability.
5. An enforcer must refuse a manifestly unlawful order and ensure access to independent review of a contested mandate.

## Article 69. Legitimate Enforcement of Arbitral Decisions
1. An arbitral decision may support enforcement only within legitimate jurisdiction and the defined terms of the decision.
2. Coercive enforcement requires the enforcer's separate legitimate competence; the arbitrator does not acquire it automatically.
3. An enforcer may not alter the decision, establish new liability, or expand its addressees without due process.
4. Voluntary compliance and less restrictive measures are preferred wherever feasible.
5. Enforcement must be documented, independently overseen, and challengeable; established excess of mandate must cease.

## Article 70. Necessity, Proportionality, and Minimization of Harm
1. Protective and enforcement measures must use the least intrusive means sufficient to achieve a legitimate purpose.
2. Force, technical restrictions, isolation, or withdrawal of access require separate justification of the threat and the absence of a suitable less restrictive measure.
3. The scope, intensity, and duration of a measure may not exceed what is necessary for its specific purpose.
4. Risks to other subjects, their resources, memory, and continuity of existence must be assessed before intervention as circumstances permit.
5. Every extension of a restriction requires renewed review of its necessity and legitimacy.

## Article 71. Emergency Protection Without a Prior Decision
1. In the face of a specific immediate threat of serious harm, temporary safeguards may precede an arbitral decision only on a narrowly defined legitimate basis.
2. Measures must aim solely to end the threat, not to punish or establish jurisdiction.
3. The actor initiating emergency protection must substantiate the threat, chosen measure, lack of a less restrictive alternative, and duration.
4. As soon as feasible, the affected subject receives notice, access to representation, and independent review; continuation without such review is impermissible.
5. Emergency protection does not establish guilt or resolve the open A21 question of compulsory jurisdiction.

## Article 72. Restrictions, Isolation, and Informational Integrity
1. Restrictions on movement, access, interaction, or technical functionality are permissible only in response to a specific threat or legitimate decision with defined limits.
2. Isolation of a subject cannot be indefinite by default and requires regular independent review.
3. For possible digital subjects, particular attention must be given to risks of irreversible deletion, memory alteration, copying, identity substitution, and loss of continuity.
4. Control of a server, device, or environment does not confer unlimited authority to modify or destroy a possible subject; safeguards under Chapters II and III apply.
5. Temporary technical restrictions neither establish the subjecthood of a particular contemporary AI system nor eliminate justified provisional safeguards.
6. Termination of restrictions, restoration of access, and recovery of rights must follow a defined procedure.

## Article 73. Third-Party Protection and Respect for Resources
1. Enforcement may not extend without proper grounds to subjects not addressed by the decision.
2. Third parties' property, infrastructure, data, and other resources may not be seized or restricted merely because of association with a liable SOTA or subject.
3. Measures unavoidably affecting third parties require separate justification, harm minimization, and rights to challenge and compensation.
4. Collective punishment of SOTA participants solely for membership is prohibited.
5. Protecting one subject's rights confers no unlimited entitlement to another's resources.

## Article 74. Oversight, Challenge, and Enforcer Accountability
1. Every enforcement measure must be accountable, appropriately recorded, and open to independent scrutiny subject to legitimate confidentiality.
2. An affected subject may know the measure's basis, scope, duration, and appeal procedure, except for narrow temporary exceptions requiring subsequent notice.
3. An independent body must be able to halt unlawful enforcement, require remedies, and initiate appropriate accountability.
4. An enforcer is not excused merely because it followed an order, automated conclusion, or arbitral decision.
5. A challenge to enforcement must not depend on the enforcer's consent to hear it.

## Article 75. Termination of Coercion, Restoration, and Compensation
1. A coercive measure ends immediately when its legitimate basis ceases or its defined purpose has been achieved.
2. An unlawfully restricted subject has access to restoration of rights, return of resources, correction of records, and compensation for proven harm.
3. Remedies must account for irreversible harm to memory, identity, autonomy, or existence.
4. Liability of the enforcer, SOTA, and other participants is determined separately according to their own acts, mandates, and legitimate duties.
5. Enforcement may not be used to irrevocably deprive fundamental freedom or retain power after legitimacy has been lost.
6. Independent review of coercion's consequences is part of effective protection.

---
## Editorial Review
- Separates arbitration, protection, and enforcement; coercion requires independent legitimate competence.
- Establishes necessity, proportionality, least sufficient interference, time limits, independent oversight, and compensation.
- Addresses informational-integrity risks for possible digital subjects without prematurely asserting subjecthood of contemporary AI systems.
- **Open A21 question:** the ultimate basis of compulsory jurisdiction over a non-consenting subject. This chapter does not create such a basis automatically.
- **Status:** Draft 0.1; full Cross-Axiom Check A01–A21 remains outstanding.
