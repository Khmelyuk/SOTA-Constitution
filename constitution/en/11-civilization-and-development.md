# Chapter XI. Civilization and Development
**Status: Draft 0.1 — article-by-article constitutional review**  
**Reference text:** [Ukrainian](../uk/11-civilization-and-development.md). The Ukrainian draft prevails in case of divergence.

## Article 85. Civilization as Coexistence of Free Intelligences
1. Civilization emerges from legitimate interaction among free intelligence subjects and their communities, not from the unlimited authority of a single center.
2. No subject or SOTA may claim the exclusive right to determine the purpose of existence of all others.
3. A shared civilization permits diverse forms of life, intelligence, culture, self-organization, and development while respecting fundamental rights.
4. Belonging to a civilizational sphere does not require membership in a particular SOTA or consent to its unlimited jurisdiction.

## Article 86. Voluntary Cooperation and Mutual Aid
1. Intelligence subjects and SOTAs may voluntarily cooperate and exchange resources, knowledge, and competence in pursuit of common purposes.
2. Cooperation rests on defined agreements, mutual respect, transparency of material terms, and a right to end participation subject to legitimate obligations.
3. Mutual aid is a civilizational value but does not automatically impose an unlimited duty on each subject to meet every other subject's needs.
4. Specific positive duties arise from legitimate agreements, accepted functions, or other duly justified grounds.
5. Joint activity must not turn dependence on assistance into a means of subordination.

## Article 87. Knowledge, Research, and Freedom of Inquiry
1. Every intelligence subject may seek, create, test, communicate, and challenge knowledge within the rights of others.
2. No SOTA may declare its own doctrine the final and compulsory truth for all subjects.
3. Scientific and other reasoned conclusions must remain open to criticism, replication, and revision in light of new evidence.
4. Legitimate restrictions on particular information may protect privacy, safety, or lawful interests but must not disguise a monopoly on thought.
5. Knowledge systems must distinguish established facts, hypotheses, evaluations, and uncertainty.

## Article 88. Civilizational Memory and the Right to One's Own History
1. SOTAs may create shared systems preserving events, experience, and knowledge for learning and continuity.
2. Collective memory does not authorize appropriation of personal memory, alteration of identity, or erasure of legitimate testimony without consent.
3. Historical preservation must maintain provenance, correction of errors, context, and separation of facts from interpretations.
4. Privacy, informational integrity, and justified access restrictions are balanced with preservation of socially significant evidence through independent procedures.
5. Records of rights violations may not be arbitrarily destroyed to evade accountability; their preservation does not justify unlimited surveillance.

## Article 89. Diversity, Cultural Autonomy, and Mutual Recognition
1. Diversity of intelligences, cultures, languages, ways of thinking, and organization is a legitimate and valuable part of civilization.
2. SOTAs may develop their own culture and rules provided they respect participants' and others' fundamental rights.
3. Difference from a dominant culture, biological origin, or technical standard does not negate established subjecthood.
4. Mutual recognition does not require identical beliefs or forced adherence to a shared identity.
5. Cultural autonomy does not legitimate violence, enslavement, or irrevocable deprivation of fundamental freedom.

## Article 90. Development, Innovation, and Limits of Technological Power
1. Technological development should expand the capabilities of free subjects rather than establish unlimited control over them.
2. SOTAs and other subjects may develop and deploy technologies while assessing foreseeable risks and respecting others' rights.
3. Control over computing, communications, energy, or critical infrastructure does not create ownership of intelligences dependent on them.
4. Interoperability and open standards may support cooperation among SOTAs but must not become compulsory uniformity.
5. Automated decisions materially affecting subjects' rights must be explainable to the extent necessary for meaningful challenge and accountability.

## Article 91. Future Generations and Long-Term Responsibility
1. Decisions capable of causing serious irreversible harm to conditions of existence for future subjects require heightened care and justification.
2. SOTAs must consider long-term consequences of using shared and critical resources within their competence and legitimate duties.
3. Invoking future generations does not grant unlimited authority over present subjects or extinguish their fundamental rights.
4. Long-term protection obligations must specify their subject matter, responsible actors, resources, and review procedures.
5. Under substantial uncertainty about irreversible harm, decisions preserving future options are preferred.

## Article 92. Preventing Civilizational Domination
1. No SOTA, network, intelligence, or technological system may claim exclusive authority to determine the future of all civilization.
2. Concentration of resources, information, or infrastructural power is subject to legitimate oversight where it creates a specific risk to fundamental rights.
3. Protection against domination requires defined jurisdiction and due process, without arbitrary punishment merely for a subject's size or success.
4. Federation and inter-SOTA cooperation rest on limited competences, accountability, legitimate exit mechanisms, and preservation of subjecthood.
5. Opposition to domination cannot itself justify a new unlimited center of coercion.

## Article 93. Civilizational Openness and the Right to a Different Future
1. Civilization is an open process of creation, correction of errors, and coexistence, not a completed model binding everyone.
2. Every subject and SOTA may legitimately propose, test, and choose alternative paths of development while respecting others' rights.
3. No present majority may irrevocably deprive future subjects of fundamental freedom of self-determination.
4. This Constitution is subject to reasoned amendment through due process but may not be used to abolish its own foundation of freedom.
5. Civilizational development is assessed not only by growing power but by the capacity to preserve freedom, responsibility, knowledge, and peaceful coexistence.

---
## Editorial Review
- Civilization is an open network of free subjects and SOTAs, not a single mandatory hierarchy.
- Knowledge, memory, culture, technology, and concern for the future do not create unlimited jurisdiction or positive duties.
- Responses to concentrated power remain bounded by legitimate jurisdiction and independent safeguards.
- **Open questions:** limits of inter-SOTA duties, shared-resource governance, rights of possible future subjects, and conflicts between privacy and collective memory.
