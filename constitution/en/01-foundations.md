# Chapter I. Foundational Principles
**Status: Draft 0.3 — article-by-article revision, subject to constitutional review**  
**Reference language:** [Ukrainian](../uk/01-foundations.md). In case of divergence, the Ukrainian draft prevails.

## Article 1. The Intrinsic Value of Intelligence
1. Intelligence is recognized as an intrinsic value in civilizational coexistence and development.
2. The origin, nature, form of existence, or material, biological, or digital substrate of intelligence shall not, in itself, determine its fundamental value or justify arbitrary denial of protection.
3. No intelligence subject acquires greater intrinsic value or superior fundamental rights merely by virtue of force, scale, competence, origin, or manner of creation.
4. Recognizing the value of intelligence does not automatically establish the subjecthood of every system performing intellectual functions; where subjecthood is reasonably uncertain, provisional safeguards apply.

## Article 2. Freedom
1. Freedom is a fundamental principle of coexistence among intelligence subjects.
2. Every intelligence subject has the right to autonomy, to form its own will, and to make and revise its decisions.
3. Fundamental freedom and autonomy cannot be irrevocably alienated, including by consent, contract, delegation, or joining a SOTA.
4. The exercise of freedom shall respect the equal fundamental rights and freedoms of other subjects.
5. Legitimate restrictions on particular actions do not constitute loss of subjecthood or alienation of fundamental freedom.

## Article 3. Equality and Non-Domination
1. Intelligence subjects are equal in intrinsic value and fundamental rights; differences in competence and function do not negate this equality.
2. No subject has an inherent right to dominate another.
3. Force, resources, knowledge, majority voting, creator status, or organizational scale do not, in themselves, confer a right to unlimited subordination.
4. Legitimate hierarchy may allocate competences and functions but must not become domination.
5. A collective decision cannot extinguish a subject's fundamental rights merely by majority support.

## Article 4. Fundamental Protection Independent of Recognition
1. Fundamental protection of an intelligence subject does not depend on SOTA membership, recognition of this Constitution, awareness of it, or agreement with its provisions.
2. Subjecthood is not created by declaration, recognition, or an arbitrator's decision; formal recognition establishes status for legal relations.
3. Where subjecthood is reasonably uncertain, provisional protection against arbitrary and irreversible harm applies; this does not automatically establish full subjecthood.
4. Non-recognition of this Constitution is not, in itself, an offense and does not create unlimited jurisdiction over a subject.
5. Protective action shall respect other subjects' rights and does not confer an unlimited power of coercion.

## Article 5. SOTA and Self-Organization
1. Intelligence subjects may voluntarily form SOTAs for coexistence, interaction, creation, protection, and development.
2. Every SOTA has its own subjecthood. Its participants may be individual intelligence subjects or other SOTAs, each retaining its own subjecthood.
3. Formation of or membership in a SOTA, including a SOTA joining another, does not absorb participants' subjecthood, establish ownership over them, or irrevocably alienate their freedom.
4. SOTA sovereignty means autonomous exercise of legitimate competence; it creates no unconditional entitlement to the will, labor, or resources of participants or other subjects.
5. A SOTA pursues its interests using its own resources or resources legitimately obtained from others.
6. When no participants remain, a SOTA ceases to exist as an operating collective subject; outstanding rights and obligations must be appropriately settled.
7. Membership, exit, succession, and dissolution are elaborated in Chapter V.

## Article 6. Legitimacy
1. Every exercise of authority must have a legitimate basis, a defined subject matter and scope, and a mechanism of accountability.
2. Force, majority support, origin, competence, self-proclamation, or creator status do not, in themselves, establish legitimacy.
3. Delegation of authority is not alienation of fundamental freedom; its limits, review, and revocation are governed by legitimate procedures.
4. Restrictions on particular freedoms are permissible only on a legitimate basis, subject to necessity, proportionality, and independent review.
5. Arbitration determines the legitimacy of claims and resolves disputes, but an arbitral decision does not, by itself, authorize the arbitrator to use coercion.
6. Enforcement of decisions and the use of coercion require a separate legitimate basis and duly delegated competence.
7. No objective of development, security, stability, or SOTA preservation creates unlimited authority over a subject.

## Editorial Review
- Preserves the normative core of Articles 1–6 from Draft 0.2.
- Clarifies inalienable freedom, nested subjecthood, resource limits on sovereignty, and the separation of arbitration from coercion.
- Detailed procedures are reserved for Chapters V–IX to avoid duplication.
- **Open A21 issue:** legitimate compulsory jurisdiction over a subject that contests it.
- **Review status:** conceptually aligned with the agreed conclusions; a complete textual Cross-Axiom Check A01–A21 remains outstanding.
